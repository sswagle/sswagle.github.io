QKD and PQC: Understanding the Quantum-Safe Security Landscape

Key message

PQC protects existing digital infrastructure against future quantum attacks; QKD provides a fundamentally different mechanism for secure key distribution. The emerging market opportunity is not simply choosing one over the other, but designing interoperable, risk-based and standards-aligned quantum-safe architectures.

QKD and PQC: Building a Quantum-Safe Future

Quantum computing will challenge widely used public-key cryptography such as RSA and ECC. Two complementary approaches are emerging: Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC): New cryptographic algorithms designed to resist quantum attacks while running on conventional IT infrastructure. NIST has standardized ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA and is driving migration toward quantum-resistant cryptography. Quantum Key Distribution (QKD): Uses quantum communication principles to establish and distribute encryption keys and detect attempts to intercept the quantum channel. ETSI and ITU-T are developing standards for QKD security, networks, protocols, interfaces and interoperability. Hybrid security: The market is increasingly looking at combining PQC, QKD and conventional cryptography to provide layered protection. IEEE is also developing standards and frameworks addressing post-quantum and quantum cybersecurity. Market direction: Organisations should begin by identifying quantum-vulnerable systems, assessing long-term data risks and developing a standards-based migration strategy. PQC is generally suitable for broad IT migration, while QKD can complement PQC for selected high-value or critical communications.

Why should the market care?

The important question is increasingly not “QKD or PQC?” but “where and how should each technology be used?” PQC is the primary migration path for most existing digital infrastructure because it can be deployed through software, protocols, certificates and hardware/firmware updates without requiring a quantum communication network. NIST recommends organisations start preparing and migrating now. QKD can provide an additional security layer for high-value links and environments where organisations want to diversify their dependence on computational assumptions. ETSI explicitly describes QKD as complementary to PQC rather than simply an alternative. Hybrid architectures are becoming important. ITU-T has studied hybrid approaches combining QKD with conventional and post-quantum mechanisms, while its newer QKD work addresses protocols, key management, security and network architecture. Interoperability is becoming a market requirement. ETSI's QKD work includes interoperable Key Management Service interfaces, while its 2026 GS QKD 020 specifies a REST-based interoperable KMS API.
